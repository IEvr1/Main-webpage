**Short answer:** Walk-in customers check the wait without calling by opening the public page of [Live Business Traffic](https://www.nexaipla.com/shoptraffic/). They see a colour-coded busy level that the shop updates with one tap, plus opening hours and announcements. No account, no app, and nobody at the counter has to pick up the phone. Homepage: [NexAIpla](https://www.nexaipla.com/).

The call lasts twenty seconds. "Are you busy? Can I come now?" It costs more than that, because it lands on the one person who is holding scissors, counting change, or reading a prescription. The customer in the chair waits while a stranger is told "about half an hour". And when the phone is not answered at all, the caller draws their own conclusion.

## Why this call exists

Nobody enjoys ringing a shop to ask about the queue. People do it because there is no other source. The opening hours on Google say whether the door is unlocked, not whether six people are sitting inside. The call is a patch for missing information, and it disappears when the information is published.

It is also a question with only four useful answers: come now, short wait, long wait, come later. A person is not needed to give one of four answers forty times a day. A page is enough, as long as someone inside the shop keeps it true.

## What takes its place

[Live Business Traffic](https://www.nexaipla.com/shoptraffic/) gives the shop a public page with four levels, LOW, MEDIUM, BUSY, and VERY BUSY, each in its own colour. The owner or the staff change the level from a private screen. The customer's page refreshes automatically, in Greek or in English.

The same page carries the weekly hours, the special closed days, and a short announcement. So the second most common call, "are you open today?", goes to the same link.

## Moving the habit from the phone to the link

A link nobody knows about replaces nothing. The people who call today found the number somewhere: on Google, in the shop window, on an old receipt. The link has to stand in each of those places, beside the number or above it.

Change the voicemail greeting as well. "To see how busy we are right now, open the link on our Google profile" turns an unanswered call into an answer. When someone calls in the middle of a rush, the staff say one sentence: "It is on our page, and it is always up to date." Said kindly twice, it teaches a regular for good.

Do not remove the number. People still need it for questions a colour cannot answer: whether a product is in stock, whether you offer a certain service, whether a child can come along.

## Three shops, the same call

1. A barber with a full bench lets the phone ring out. The greeting points to the link, the caller opens it, sees BUSY, and comes after lunch instead of giving up.
2. A pharmacy assistant used to answer "is there a queue?" between prescriptions. With the link on the door sign and on Google, the phone is left for questions about medicines.
3. A café beside a school gets the same call every afternoon from parents who want a table at pick-up time. The owner taps MEDIUM at 15:30 and the parents look before they park.

## What changes at the counter

- The person serving is not pulled away for a twenty-second question
- An unanswered call no longer reads as "closed"
- Hours, closed days, and busy level under one link
- The phone stays free for questions that need a person

## How to tell whether it worked

Before you publish the link, keep a tally for one week: one stroke on paper for every call that asks only about the wait or the hours. Keep the same tally in the third week after launch. The page is working if the strokes drop while the number of people through the door does not.

If the strokes do not drop, look at two things before you blame the page. Is the link really visible wherever the number is visible? And is the level being updated, or did customers arrive on LOW and find a queue? One wrong green sends people straight back to the phone.

## Order of work

1. Count the "are you busy?" and "are you open?" calls for one normal week.
2. Have the page set up with your hours and closed days, and save the owner link on the phones of the people who work the floor.
3. Place the public link beside the phone number on Google, on social profiles, on the door, and on receipts if you print them.
4. Record a voicemail greeting that sends callers to the link.
5. Repeat the count in week three and compare.
