Where the nature of the data requires it, there are options for running on your own premises or fully offline. Where the data will live is discussed before anything starts. The worry is reasonable, and shows you are thinking straight. A small business's customer list was built over fifteen years. Its terms with suppliers are its margin. Nobody should hand those over without knowing where they go.

## Three questions hidden in one

When someone asks "is my data safe?", they usually mean three different things.

Whose it is. Can anybody else use it for a purpose of their own?

Where it is processed. On my computer, or on someone else's?

Who sees it. Which people, and which systems, have access?

It is worth separating them, because the answer is not always the same for all three.

## Whose it is

Yours. Your customers' details, your documents, your prices are yours before the project, during it, and after.

A tool made for your business uses them to do the job you asked for. There is no reason for them to serve anything else.

## Where it is processed

Here there are options, and they depend on what kind of data you have.

For many tasks, an internet service is the simplest and cheapest solution. You need no equipment, and the tool works from anywhere.

For sensitive data, there is the option of the tool running on your own computer, inside your premises, or even with no connection at all. The records never leave the building.

The second option has a price, in equipment and in flexibility. The pros and cons are set out in [AI on your premises or offline](/en/articles/on-premise-offline-ai-small-business).

## Who sees it

That is determined by how the tool is set up. The general principle is least access: whoever is needed for the job to be done sees it, and nobody else.

Ask it explicitly, and ask for the answer in writing.

## You need not send data in order to ask

Many overlook this. To receive the 48-hour feasibility reply, you describe the job. You do not attach the customer list.

"I have about two thousand customers in a spreadsheet, with name, phone, and purchase history" is enough. We do not need to see the two thousand names to tell you whether it can be done.

If examples are needed later, that is discussed then, along with how they will be supplied.

## Which data needs care in a small business

Customers' contact details and history.

Purchase prices and suppliers' terms.

Payroll and staff details.

Contracts, quotes, financial figures.

And anything concerning health, if you are a medical office, a pharmacy, or a gym.

If the task you want to automate touches any of these, say so in the description. It changes the recommendation.

## Six questions for any supplier, and for us

Where exactly will my data be?

Is it used for anything beyond my own task?

Who has access to it?

What happens to it if we stop working together?

Can I get it back in a form that opens?

How and when is it deleted?

A serious partner answers all six without hesitation. If someone dodges one, you have learned something useful.

## Less is safer

The best protection is not to hand over what is not needed.

A tool that sends reminders needs a name, a mobile number, and a date. It does not need the purchase history. A tool that sorts invoices does not need the payroll.

In design, the question "which fields does it really need?" comes before the question "where shall we put them?".

## Your own obligations remain

Towards your customers, the one responsible for their details is you. That does not change because a tool processes them.

If you have specific obligations, from your sector or from contracts with clients, mention them from the start. It is far easier to design something correctly than to correct it.

## An example from the ready-made applications

[DocsApp](https://www.nexaipla.com/docsapp/) shows what the offline option looks like in practice. It runs on a Windows computer inside the office, and the documents are uploaded nowhere. The same logic can be applied to bespoke tools too.

## When the project ends

Agree before you start what will happen at the end. What remains with you, what is deleted, in what form you get back whatever was created.

It is a five-minute conversation at the start that prevents a difficult one at the end.

## Three businesses, three decisions

1. An accounting office wants help with client documents and chooses a solution that runs inside the office, with no connection.
2. A clothes shop wants automatic answers about hours and returns. No customer details are involved, and an internet service is enough.
3. A gym describes its need without sending the members' list, and receives a feasibility reply from the description alone.

## What you secure

- The business's records remain yours
- A choice of where processing happens
- A first reply without sending a single file
- A design that uses only what is needed

## How you protect your data from the start

1. Describe the job without attaching real records.
2. State what kind of data the task touches.
3. Ask where processing will happen and who will have access.
4. Ask for only the necessary fields to be used.
5. Agree in writing what happens to the data at the end.
