The schedule of a service business is not a grid. It is a puzzle of pieces of different sizes, each of which fits only certain hands. When you squeeze it into equal boxes, you either lose time or chase the clock all day.

## Why fixed half hours fail

A forty-five-minute service on a half-hour grid takes two boxes. Fifteen minutes are lost. Six of those in a day make an hour and a half that you pay for and do not sell.

The opposite is worse. A forty-minute service written into one half-hour box eats ten minutes of the next. By five in the afternoon you are an hour behind, with a waiting room of people looking at the clock.

## The service list is the foundation

Everything starts from a list: what you offer and how long it takes.

Write it honestly. Not how long you would like a haircut to take, but how long it takes on an ordinary Tuesday, with the wash, the chat, and the payment.

If you are not sure, time a week. Most professionals underestimate their durations by ten minutes.

## Put in the time between as well

Cleaning the station, changing the sheet on the massage table, disinfecting the tools. That time exists whether you write it down or not.

The simplest way is to include it in the service's duration. The fifty-minute massage is declared as one hour.

## Who does what

Not everyone does everything. One specialises in colour, another in beards, a third does nails only. In a physiotherapy practice, a particular treatment is given by one therapist alone.

At setup you match each team member to their services. When the customer chooses a colour, only those who colour are suggested.

That stops a mistake that happens often on the phone: the appointment made with someone who was free but does not do that job.

## How the times come out

The system combines three things. The service's duration. The appointments the person already has. And the closures you have declared.

From those it calculates where there is a gap large enough, and offers those times. A half-hour gap is offered for a haircut and is not offered for a colour.

The calculation is done at the moment the customer asks, so it includes the appointment made a minute ago.

## The same service, a different pace

The experienced hand finishes in half an hour what the junior does in fifty minutes. If the difference is large, do not sweep it under the carpet.

A practical way is to declare them as two separate services, with different durations, each matched to its own person. Often they have a different price anyway.

Tell us at setup how your team works, so that we arrange it in the way that fits.

## Combined services

Colour and cut. Manicure and pedicure. Customers ask for them together.

If a combination is frequent, make it a service with its own name and total duration. The customer selects it with one tap and the gap reserved is the right one.

## Few services, clear names

The choice is made in a chat, on a phone. A list of forty lines is tiring.

Group them. If three services have the same duration and are done by the same people, from the schedule's point of view they are one.

And write names the customer understands, not the ones you use among yourselves. The customer does not know what "technical 2" is.

## A review after a month

The first durations are estimates. After four weeks, look at where you ran late and where you waited.

If you were rushing every afternoon, some service has been declared shorter than reality. If you often had ten minutes with nothing to do, one has been declared longer.

The correction concerns future appointments. Those already made stay at the time the customer knows.

## When the team or the list changes

A new service: it is added with a duration and the people who do it.

A new member: they are added with their services, and connect their Google Calendar.

Someone has trained in something new: you add the service to them, and from that moment they are suggested to customers.

## Three settings from practice

1. A salon declares a haircut at 30 minutes and a colour at 120. A 45-minute gap is offered for a haircut only.
2. A beauty institute matches nails to two of its four people. Whoever asks for a manicure sees only those two.
3. A massage studio declares the 50-minute session as 60, to have time to prepare the room.

## What you gain from correct durations

- The schedule matches what actually happens
- Fewer lost ten-minute gaps, fewer delays
- The customer books only with someone who does the service
- Changes to the list pass into availability at once

## How you set up the list

1. Write your services with names the customer understands.
2. Time them for a week and add preparation time.
3. Note next to each who does it.
4. Merge frequent combinations into one service.
5. Review the durations after four weeks.
