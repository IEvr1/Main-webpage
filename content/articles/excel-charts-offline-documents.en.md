Every small business has one Excel file that knows everything. Sales per day, costs per supplier, staff hours. And almost always only one person can read it: the one who made it. The rest open it, see four thousand rows, and close it.

## Why Excel goes unused

To get a chart out of a spreadsheet you need to know three things: where the data is, how to summarise it, and how the chart is made. The owner knows the first. The accountant or the nephew knows the other two.

So questions stay unanswered. Which month was the worst? Which supplier went up most? The data exists. The answer is fifteen minutes of work away, which nobody will do.

## What changes when you ask in plain language

You drop the file into the intake folder and write the question as you would say it: "Show me sales by month for this year." The application reads the columns, works out which is the date and which the amount, and gives you the chart.

You write no formulas and build no pivot table. If the chart is not what you wanted, you say so: "By week, not by month", or "only the Limassol shop".

## Which questions fit

Totals and averages. "What was the total of expenses in March?"

Comparisons. "Compare Monday and Saturday sales."

Ranking. "Who are the five customers with the highest turnover?"

Trends over time. "How did energy cost move over the last twelve months?"

Charts of all the above: bars, lines, pies, according to what shows the answer best.

## Why locally

A sheet of sales is among the most sensitive files a business has. It contains your turnover, often customer names, sometimes salaries. Online analysis tools ask you to upload it.

Here the file stays on your disk. The model that reads it runs on the same computer. The chart is made there and appears in your browser at 127.0.0.1:8080, which is your own machine.

## The file has to help

The AI reads what is there. If the sheet is untidy, the answers will match. Five simple habits make a big difference.

One row of headings at the top, with clear names: "Date", "Amount", "Category". Not "Column1".

One record per row. No merged cells, no subtotals among the data.

Dates as dates, not as text like "early March".

Amounts as numbers, without the euro sign typed into the cell.

One table per sheet. Three small tables side by side confuse both people and models.

## Always check the number

A chart persuades easily, and for that reason needs care. Before resting a decision on it, run a quick check: ask for the total and compare it with what you know from your accounting software.

If March's turnover in the chart is half what you remember, something was read wrongly: a column, a filter, a date in another format. Ask "which columns did you use?" and you will find it.

The tool is an analysis assistant, not an accountant. Your returns and balance sheets continue to come from your accounting system.

## Together with the other documents

Excel files live in the same application as your PDFs and Word files. You can have in the same folder the contract with a supplier and the sheet with their invoices, and ask first what the contract says about price adjustment and then how the prices really moved.

Conversations are saved as threads. Next week you find the analysis again without repeating it.

## Speed and size

The models run on the processor, with no graphics card. A sheet of a few thousand rows is analysed comfortably. The larger the file, the more patience is needed.

## Three questions to spreadsheets

1. A café owner asks "which day of the week has the lowest turnover?" and sees a bar chart with Tuesday at the bottom.
2. An accountant asks for "expenses by category for the second quarter" from a client's CSV export and checks the total against the trial balance.
3. A warehouse manager asks which ten items moved least this year and gets the list as a table.

## How you begin

1. Pick an Excel file you really use.
2. Tidy it: one row of headings, one record per row.
3. Drop it into the intake folder.
4. Ask for a total you already know, as a check.
5. Then ask for the chart you never had time to make.
