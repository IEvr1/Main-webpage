Your hours and your customers' hours coincide almost exactly. While you work, so do they. The moment they have a free mind to organise a haircut, a massage, or a service is precisely the moment you have pulled the shutters down.

## When the customer decides

In the evening, once the children are asleep. On Sunday, when planning the week. In the lunch break, when you have a customer in the chair and your hands are busy.

That last one deserves attention. "After hours" for a small business is not only the night. It is every moment you cannot pick up the phone, and in a shop of one or two people that holds for most hours of the day.

## What happens to those moments today

The customer phones, nobody answers, and they hang up. Some leave a message. Most do not.

Others send a message on social media. You see it in the morning, reply "what time suits you?", they answer at noon, you in the afternoon. The appointment is made after a day and four messages, if it is made.

Meanwhile, their mood may have changed, or they may have found another shop that answered first. The phenomenon is described in detail in [why customers are lost after hours](/en/articles/why-businesses-miss-leads-after-office-hours).

## Hiring does not add up

A person at reception solves the problem of busy hands, not of the night. Nobody will sit at a barber's till at eleven at night.

And for daytime hours, a wage so that the phone gets picked up is an expense few shops of two or three people can bear.

In practice, the receptionist is the owner, with scissors in one hand.

## What the page does at eleven at night

Whatever a person would do, apart from the chat.

It asks what service the customer wants and with whom. It shows the times that are free at that moment. They choose, give a mobile number, and the appointment is made.

The confirmation SMS arrives at once. The customer puts the phone down knowing that on Thursday at six they have an appointment.

## A completed booking, not a request

That is the difference from a contact form or a message. Those create work for the morning: to reply, to propose a time, to wait.

Here you have nothing to do in the morning. The appointment exists. The time is reserved, so nobody else will take it. The customer already knows.

## What you find in the morning

You open the board and see the day and the week, with the appointments added while you were away. Each team member sees theirs in their Google Calendar as well.

Some days you will find cancellations too. Those also happened by themselves overnight, from the link in the SMS, and the hours have already gone back into availability.

## Holidays and time off

The page is always open. The shop is not. That is why you declare closures on the board: the mid-August holiday, your name day, the week you are away.

The customer booking from the beach in August sees no times for the days you are closed. They see the first Monday you open, and fill it.

That is perhaps the most useful result: you come back from holiday to a full schedule, not to forty missed calls.

## So that they find you at night

The page does not help if the customer does not know it exists. The link has to be where someone looks at ten at night.

On your map profile, where today it says "Closed".

At the top of your social media profile.

In the automatic reply to your messages, if you have one.

In the window, as a QR code, for whoever walks past.

## What it does not do

It does not answer questions that need an opinion. "Which colour suits me?" and "is it worth repairing?" wait for you.

It does not pick up the phone or call back.

It does not serve the customer who does not want to use a mobile. That one will phone when you open, as always, and you will book them from the same page.

## How you will know it works

For a month, count each morning how many new appointments you found waiting. Those are appointments that used to be missed calls, pending messages, or nothing.

2. A barber is cutting hair and the phone rings. The customer sees the link on the shop's profile and books by himself.
