Many owners of small businesses never ask. Not because they have no problem, but because they think they must first learn the language. They read two articles full of acronyms, feel that this is not for them, and go back to the notebook.

## What you know that nobody else does

Your job.

You know that orders come in three ways and that the third causes most of the mistakes. You know which customer always changes their mind on Friday. You know that the accountant's program wants dates in a particular way.

That knowledge is the hard part, and it is in no manual. The technical part can be brought by someone else. Yours cannot.

## What you need not know

What the models are called and which is better.

What a programming interface is and whether your program has one.

Whether what you are asking for is "artificial intelligence" or plain software. Often the right solution is something far simpler than you imagined, and it is our job to say so.

How code is written. Not one line.

## Tell it as the story of an ordinary day

The best way of describing is not technical. It is narrative.

"Every morning I open the email and find ten to fifteen orders from shops. Each is written differently. I enter them one by one in the spreadsheet, and then write the slip for the warehouse. It takes me an hour and a half. Mistakes happen when someone writes cases and I read pieces."

That paragraph says everything: where the information comes from, in what form, what you do to it, where it goes, how long it takes, and where it breaks. It contains not one technical word.

## Four things worth mentioning

What business you run and how big it is. Two people or twenty, a shop or an office.

The problem. One, the most annoying.

What success looks like to you. "Not opening the spreadsheet in the morning" is a perfect answer.

The tools you already have. By the names you know them by. "The till program", "an Excel file my nephew made".

## By voice, if that comes more easily

Some people think better by talking. That is why the voice message option exists.

Two minutes, from the car or behind the counter when the shop empties. As you would say it to a friend who asked "what bothers you at work?".

It need not be organised. It needs to be true.

## Two descriptions of the same need

The first: "I want an AI system for process optimisation and digital transformation."

The second: "I have a garage with three mechanics. Customers phone me to ask whether the car is ready, twenty times a day, and each time I drop what I am doing."

The first sounds professional and says nothing. The second is the one that can be answered.

## Translation is our job

From your story we draw the technical part: what kind of tool is needed, whether it must connect to something you have, where there is risk.

The reply comes back to you in your own language. A feasibility percentage, an indicative cost range, a time estimate, recommended next steps. If a technical term is needed somewhere, it is explained.

## "Is my problem perhaps silly?"

That is the question that holds most people back.

The problems worth automating are rarely impressive. They are small and boring, and repeat thirty times a day. Copying from one place to another. The same answer to the same question. The reminder that gets forgotten.

If it irritates you every day, it is not silly. In [which company automates tasks with AI](/en/articles/company-that-automates-business-tasks-with-ai) you will find examples of such tasks.

## If you do not understand something in the reply

You ask. There is no obligation to go ahead, and no question is considered too basic.

A reply the owner does not understand is our failure, not theirs.

## And afterwards? Shall I be able to use it?

A reasonable worry. A tool for a business of five people must work without a manual.

That is why it helps to tell us who will use it. You? The employee at the till? Your mother who keeps the books? The answer changes how it has to be made.
